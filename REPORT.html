The 300% Problem

Eighty-seven strategies on the BTCUSDT perpetual, against a target of 300% net annual profit at under 20% drawdown. One account, one position at a time. This is the record of what was tested, what survived walk-forward validation, and the measured reason the target stays out of reach — which turns out not to be signal quality at all, but which year you are standing in.

Verdict

No strategy qualified.

The best strategy returns 179.0% net annual at a 19.99% drawdown over four and a half years including the 2022 bear market — five signals reduced to one position, with the conviction curve and a trend gate re-chosen every quarter on trailing data, three configurations held at once. Profit factor 3.18, 1,769 trades, Sharpe 2.13, Calmar 8.95. It passes every gate except the return, and misses that by 1.7×. At a size the bootstrap calls survivable it returns 86.2% at −12.3%. The gains came from four places and not one was a better signal: less correlated signals, the shape of the bet, matching the selection objective to the target, and refusing to short a market that is trending up. Section 12 shows why all four run out.

Three things this table is not telling you

V2's window contains no bear market. Its 24-month selection lookback pushes the first tradeable quarter to 2023-03, and the panel cannot begin before 2021-03 because BTC dominance and positioning data both start 2021-01. So V2 competes with V4 on return while never having traded 2022. That is not a fair fight, and it is why V3 — the same machinery on a 12-month lookback, measured on the harder sample — exists at all.

Every drawdown here is marked on daily closes. The quarterly books are assembled from per-quarter segments and have no continuous intraday equity curve, so daily marks are the only measure all seven share. It is mildly optimistic: V1 measured on its own 15-minute curve draws down 14.9% where daily marks say 12.7%. Read every drawdown as "on daily closes" and add roughly two points for the path within the day.

V7 fits one number the others do not. The blend width k was read off a curve that was flat from k=3 to k=12, which is why a mid-range k rather than the argmax was adopted — but it is still a parameter chosen with the whole sample in view. The trend gate underneath it is not: it was demoted to an axis of the quarterly grid and chosen causally, from a menu of four spans, of which two were never inspected.

4 — What actually moved the number

Correlation, not signal quality

The best strategy improved from 22% to 51% a year at the same drawdown during this study. Not one point of that came from finding a stronger signal. It came from finding signals that were different — and the effect is large enough, and regular enough, to be the report's main result.

Netting beats running the signals as separate books

For most of this study the signals ran as separate sub-accounts that rebalanced monthly. That is not tradable in a single account, so the whole thing was rebuilt as one strategy: the signals are averaged into a single number and the account holds one position at a time. The rebuild was expected to cost something — each signal loses its own stop and its own exit clock, and blending had degraded the strongest signal three separate times earlier in this study.

It gained instead. Profit factor went from 1.51 to 2.09 and Sharpe from 1.84 to 2.13 on the same window, because opposing signals now cancel before anything is traded rather than both being opened and both paying a round turn. The three earlier blending failures all averaged correlated signals, where the average is just a noisier version of the same view. Averaging signals that correlate 0.10 is a different operation.

The second lever: the shape of the bet, not the size of it

Once the signals were saturated, the arithmetic left exactly one term. CAGR at the drawdown limit = (Calmar/Sharpe) × Sharpe × 20%. Sharpe is capped near 3 by the correlation ceiling. The bet count is closed — running the same signals at 6h, 4h, 2h and 1h decisions gives up to eight times the trades and collapses profit factor from 1.81 to 0.99, monotonically, because sampling a twelve-hourly signal faster manufactures threshold crossings out of noise. That leaves the ratio, which was 1.68 — and unlike the other two, it is not a property of the market. It is a property of how the bet is shaped.

Sizing on |net|² instead of |net| — betting far harder when all five signals agree, rescaled so the average position is unchanged, so this is shape and not leverage — holds the drawdown flat and lifts everything else. Pushing the exponent further keeps lifting the ratio:

The third lever: rank by what you are actually maximising

For the whole adaptive phase the quarterly re-selection ranked configurations by their trailing Sharpe. Sharpe is close to size-invariant, so it chose the shape of the bet and was blind to drawdown — while the target being chased is Calmar. That mismatch sat unexamined in one line of code, and fixing it was worth more than the last several structural experiments put together.

Same return at half the risk, and profit factor 2.71 → 3.26 — the highest of any headline book here. Eighteen selection rules were compared and the whole 24-month drawdown-aware family won together (Calmar 8.11, 8.11, 7.76 on the fast screen) rather than one lucky cell, which is the main defence against having merely overfitted the meta-parameters. Blending the top 2, 3 or 5 configurations instead of taking the best lost every time — the third occasion in this study where blending lost to picking.

One thing this rule could not choose is position size. Since Calmar, unlike Sharpe, is not size-invariant, letting the search pick the risk level should locate the growth-optimal size from data. It picked the top of the grid in all fourteen quarters, producing 345% CAGR at a −37% drawdown with a 99% chance of breaching the limit. That is leverage, not edge, and it fails the brief by nearly 2×. The saturation is a window artefact: on a stretch with no bear market, Calmar rises with leverage and never turns over, so a drawdown-based objective cannot find the optimum on data that never punishes leverage. Size is the one parameter that cannot be delegated — it has to come from the drawdown the operator can actually tolerate.

The right exponent is regime-dependent, so it is chosen quarterly

Walk-forward resolved the divergence: the early folds want 1.0, the later ones 2.5–3.0. Neither fixed choice is right for the whole sample, and re-choosing every quarter from the trailing eighteen months uses no future information — so it is implementable, not just a diagnostic.

The engine was throwing away information it already had

Every signal produces a z-score, and the entry logic collapsed it to ±1 — so a 3σ reading and a 1.01σ reading placed the same bet. Letting |z| scale the risk budget, capped at 2× so no single reading can take the account, raised out-of-sample Sharpe on all four books it was tested on: implied volatility 1.41 → 1.61, stablecoin basis 0.99 → 1.27, order flow 1.22 → 1.26, dominance 1.11 → 1.13.

The instructive part is the contrast. Conviction filtering — trading only readings beyond 1.5× the threshold — roughly halves Sharpe on every book (1.41 → 0.92, 1.11 → 0.25). So the extremes do not carry the edge; the edge is monotone in |z| and the ordering is the information. That is what a real information coefficient looks like from the inside, and it is a stronger check on these signals than any single backtest. The same lesson killed two other ideas: a dead band on the net signal, and replacing ATR with a HAR volatility forecast for stop placement, which lost on all four books.

The one input class the study had never touched

Every signal in this study up to this point was realised: prices, taker flow, open interest, trader positioning, funding. Binance also publishes BVOL, a 30-day forward-looking implied volatility index for BTC — a VIX analogue, at one-second resolution. 1,152 daily files were streamed and reduced to 1,656,492 minute bars covering 2023-06-20 → 2026-09-09. It is not a measurement of past risk. It is the market's price of future risk.

The variance risk premium is right there and it is real — mean implied volatility 51.4% against mean realised 42.7%, a +8.7 point premium, the same anomaly that exists in equities. It is also not harvestable here: collecting it means selling options, and this is a futures study.

What is tradable is the movement. Most implied-volatility features are unstable — the level, the premium and the implied/realised ratio all flip sign between in- and out-of-sample. One does not: the three-day change in implied volatility carries a rank-IC of +0.046 in-sample and +0.091 out-of-sample at a one-day horizon, and it is not a price-momentum proxy — the two correlate −0.10, and price momentum's own IC over this window is negative. Projecting price momentum out raises the IC to +0.083 rather than lowering it. Decile spread: +44 bps against a 16 bps round turn, the widest margin over cost of anything in the study.

5 — The binding constraint

Compound growth has a maximum

The intuition that a good strategy can simply be levered to any return target is wrong, and measurably so. Below the growth-optimal size more risk buys more compound return; above it, volatility drag and stop-outs mean more risk buys less return and strictly more drawdown. Each curve below is the same strategy run at twelve position sizes.

What the curve says

The convex trend book returns +59.6% at 5% risk per trade. At 22% risk — the kind of sizing a 300% target would demand — the same strategy on the same data returns −71.6% with a 100% drawdown. There is no setting in between that reaches 300%, because the peak of the curve is roughly 60% and the peak is a property of the return distribution, not of the parameters.

Under a 20% drawdown budget you are operating at the far left of these curves, around 1–2% risk, which is where the ~23% figures come from. The drawdown limit and the return target are pulling in opposite directions along a curve whose maximum is already an order of magnitude below the target.

7 — Reproduction

Complete specifications

Everything needed to rebuild the top books. Rolling windows are counted in decision bars. Costs and execution rules are identical across every strategy and listed once in section 13. An earlier version of this work ran the signals as separate sub-accounts rebalancing monthly; that structure is not tradable in one account and, when rebuilt as a single net position, was beaten outright — profit factor 1.51 against 2.09. The sub-account results are kept in the research log but are not specified here, because they are not what anyone should trade.

The fast end, measured and closed from two directions

The order book was the last classic source. 24 days of top-of-book quotes were streamed and reduced to 34,547 minute bars (2023-06 → 2024-02, the whole window Binance publishes). Market context, measured rather than assumed: mean quoted spread 0.034 bps, mean top-of-book depth $582,481, 11,744 quote updates per minute.

Order-book imbalance at the minute's last quote is the strongest and most stable predictor found anywhere in this study — rank-IC +0.108 in the early blocks and +0.081 in the late ones, holding both its sign and most of its magnitude, which almost nothing else here did. And it is completely untradable: the decile spread is +0.16 bps at one minute and +1.23 bps at sixty, against a 16 bps round turn. Two orders of magnitude short. Even at maker fees it does not clear. This is the study's cleanest separation of statistical significance from economic significance. Order-book imbalance is a market maker's signal — they are paid the spread for providing liquidity, and imbalance tells them which way to skew. A taker must cross that spread, and no amount of predictive accuracy at a one-minute horizon recovers 16 bps from a 0.16 bps move.

Two things that did check out

Execution granularity. Every result resolved stops on 15-minute bars with the stop assumed to fill first when a bar straddled both levels. Re-run on the full 1-minute history (3,506,400 bars, zero gaps): S7 goes 22.9% → 21.8%, and the convex book's drawdown improves from −36.0% to −30.5% because the worst-case rule fires less often on a finely resolved path. The 15-minute grid was mildly optimistic on return and pessimistic on drawdown. No conclusion changes.

Parameter ensembling. Running all 72 configurations of the S7 book simultaneously at 1/N size, rather than selecting one, lifts Sharpe from a median single-config 1.34 to 1.54 in sample and 0.73 to 0.80 out of sample. Single configurations ranged from −0.6% to +36.0% CAGR out of sample; that spread is precisely the selection risk the grid search exposed. This is the right default. It does not move the ceiling.

Averaging dilutes, and gating cannot rescue

Equal-weight averaging dragged the strongest signal toward the weakest twice — S2 took profit factor from 1.30 to 1.19, and blending breadth into the positioning composite took IC from 0.108 to 0.070. Conditional gating is the usual answer, and it worked in S1 (profit factor 1.04 to 1.30). Here it did not: every gate that actually removed trades hurt, and the one variant matching the baseline vetoed just 12 of 229 trades — it converges to doing nothing. Gating works when the confirmer is genuinely predictive. Breadth was not, and no combination rule rescues a signal with no out-of-sample edge.

The sharpest methodological lesson

The breadth signal's in-sample decile economics were the most convincing in the whole study outside the winner: top decile +131.9 bps over 96 hours, t = 10.9, +115.9 bps net of cost, and only 0.22 correlated with the incumbent. Traded, it lost money in every out-of-sample variant. A t-statistic of 10.9 computed on overlapping 96-hour windows is far weaker evidence than it appears, and in-sample decile economics are not evidence of out-of-sample tradability.

11 — Validation

Does it survive being chosen honestly?

A single in-sample/out-of-sample split tests one decision boundary. It does not test the process that made the choices — and by this point the study had made a lot of them, several fixed early and carried forward, so the whole sample had influenced them. This re-runs the selection properly.

The 5.5 years are cut into eight rolling folds, eighteen months of training and six months of testing. On each training window a 54-point grid — threshold scale × stop multiple × reward-to-risk × holding cap — is searched and the best configuration picked by in-window Sharpe alone. That configuration is then applied, untouched, to the six months immediately after. Test windows are concatenated into one curve. Nothing in a test window ever informs its own parameters.

Portfolio theory stops describing this book — and that changes what to search for

Standard practice says a signal earns its place if its own Sharpe beats its correlation to the book times the book's Sharpe: sᵢ > ρᵢ · S. Tested against every signal this study has, in the book and rejected alike, the correlation between what that criterion predicts and what actually happens is +0.05. The reason is structural, and it invalidates a chunk of this study's own earlier reasoning. A netted single-position book is not a portfolio in the mean-variance sense. The signals combine before a position exists: two agreeing make it bigger, two disagreeing make it zero. That is a nonlinear interaction through the entry, sizing and exit logic, and covariance algebra does not describe it. The correlation arithmetic that drove the middle of this study was valid for sub-accounts, which really were separate return streams — and it kept being applied after the switch to netting, where it does not hold.

The two highlighted rows are the ones to look at. BTC dominance and funding have standalone Sharpe of 0.01 and −0.04 — no edge at all, either of them — and removing either costs the book 0.17 and 0.24 of Sharpe. Their value is in vetoing: cancelling other signals' positions at the right moments. No screen of standalone performance would ever have found them, and this study's first screen didn't.

So all 116 candidates were re-screened inside the book, and it is saturated

Each feature was added to the five-signal net position one at a time and the book's Sharpe measured, ranked on in-sample only. Eight of 116 improve it — 7%. The median candidate makes it worse by 0.28 of Sharpe.

If additions were neutral you would expect about half to help. Seven percent means adding almost anything to a well-formed netted book actively hurts it — the exact mirror image of the sub-account result earlier in this report, where going from three sleeves to eight raised Sharpe monotonically. Under netting each extra signal dilutes the weight of the good ones and adds veto noise. Netting saturates, and five signals is where.

Seven of the eight in-sample improvers degrade the full sample. The one that does not is dc_pos — Donchian channel position, long near the top of the trailing 55-bar range. At matched bootstrap risk it is worth about five points of CAGR, lifting out-of-sample profit factor from 2.04 to 2.16 and Calmar from 3.68 to 4.16, and its out-of-sample gain (+0.10 Sharpe) is larger than its in-sample one (+0.023) — the right signature. It was also the 7th of 232 tested variants on a +0.023 margin, which is squarely inside the multiple-testing zone, so it is recorded as a verified-but-marginal sixth signal rather than promoted. There is a pleasing detail in which one it is: the book is built entirely of non-price reads — flow, stablecoin basis, turnover rotation, funding, positioning — and out of 116 candidates the only thing that adds to it is the simplest price signal there is.

The random row is the one that matters

Drawing a configuration at random from the same grid on every fold still returns Sharpe 2.03. The performance is coming from the signals, not from the parameter choices — the grid search is worth about 0.25 of Sharpe over random, which is real but small. A result that survives random parameterisation is not a parameter artefact, and that is a stronger statement than any single out-of-sample number in this report.

Six of the eight folds preferred a configuration that is not the published one — 2.5 ATR stopped, 3R target, held 14 days, against the published 3.0 ATR, 2R, 21 days. Run head to head on the full sample at matched bootstrap risk, the gap is 0.03 of Sharpe, and it runs the other way on out-of-sample profit factor (1.92 against 2.04). The published configuration stands: switching on a difference that small, after the fact, is precisely the overfitting this report keeps documenting. The conclusion is not that one setting beats the other — it is that the strategy is insensitive to both, which is what the random control already said.

12 — Why the target is out of reach

The arithmetic behind the shortfall

The answer changed twice, and the final one is a number

The first answer was the growth-optimal ceiling in section 4: compound return peaks and then declines, so leverage cannot be the route. That still holds — every book in this study has such a peak. But it turned out not to be the binding constraint, because the peak moves when the strategy's Sharpe rises, and Sharpe rose from 1.07 to 2.13 during this study.

The real constraint is the one underneath it. Combine k signals of individual Sharpe s with average pairwise correlation ρ̄, and the combined Sharpe is bounded — not by how many you add, but by ρ̄:

Sharpe(k) = s·√k ⁄ √(1 + (k−1)·ρ̄) → s ⁄ √ρ̄ as k → ∞

Measured on this instrument: s ≈ 0.8–1.3 per signal, ρ̄ ≈ 0.10 across the set. That puts the ceiling at Sharpe ≈ 3. Five signals reach 2.21 — about three quarters of the way — and the in-book re-screen of all 116 candidates found that only 7% of them improve the book at all, with the median candidate making it worse by 0.28 of Sharpe. Signals six through twenty are worth the remaining quarter between them, and then nothing. ρ̄ is a property of the market, not of the search — every signal available on one instrument is ultimately a view on the same price.

Where the ceiling comes from

Sharpe ≈ IC × √(independent bets per year)

The strongest predictor found was top-trader-versus-retail positioning at rank-IC 0.108 on a 96-hour horizon. One instrument at that horizon gives about 8,766 ÷ 96 ≈ 91 non-overlapping bets a year, so 0.108 × √91 ≈ 1.03 — almost exactly the Sharpe of 1.14 the strategy actually delivered. The model is not being pessimistic; it is describing what happened.

Three of the four routes to more breadth on one instrument stayed closed. Shorter horizons: IC collapses to 0.013 at 1h, and the 1h book's apparent Sharpe advantage was in-sample only (1.48 → 0.72 out of sample). Cheaper fills: worth 1.2 percentage points at these holding periods, and the maker experiment below shows why that does not change at any speed. Convexity: raises profit factor to 1.91, leaves Sharpe at 0.95.

The fourth route opened. The original four perp books correlated 0.25–0.70 because they were all directional reads on the same price, and Sharpe stayed at 1.10. Reading different markets about the same asset — the options market's implied volatility, the stablecoin basis between the USDT- and coin-margined contracts, BTC's share of turnover across the perp complex — drops the average correlation to 0.10 and takes Sharpe to 2.13 over five and a half years, 2.26 on the window where implied volatility exists. That is a real doubling, and it is where every point of this study's improvement came from. It is also finite, for the reason above.

And the grid search proves the danger of pushing harder

S16 searched 480 configurations under an explicit drawdown budget and selected on in-sample data. Under a 40% budget it found an in-sample CAGR of 73.3%. Out of sample that configuration returned 20.2% with a 54% drawdown. Under a 20% budget: in-sample 31.1%, out-of-sample 11.0%, and the drawdown breached to 23%. This is what "trying harder" produces once the real edge is exhausted — larger in-sample numbers and no out-of-sample improvement.

What 300% at 20% drawdown would actually require

A Calmar ratio of 15. The best measured here is 8.95, and it took four levers to get there — five near-independent signals to reach Sharpe 2.4, an adaptive conviction curve to push the Calmar/Sharpe ratio from 1.68 to 2.8, ranking the quarterly selection by Calmar rather than Sharpe, and refusing to short a market trading above its long moving average. The ungated single-configuration book on the same bear-inclusive window runs at 5.27, so the last two levers together are worth about 70% of the Calmar — and still leave the target a factor of 1.7 away.

But section 9 reframes what the shortfall even is. The book's return tracks market trendiness at +0.61, and in its best twelve months — sized to the same 20% limit — it returned about 408%. The target is not beyond the strategy. It is beyond the strategy's average year, and the one variable that would let you size into the good years mean-reverts at exactly the horizon you would need it (90-day trendiness against the next 90 days: −0.211).

Decomposed, 15 needs Sharpe × ratio = 15. The ratio reached 2.67 and the exponent sweep shows it flattening above 2.2 for any fixed curve. At that ratio the required Sharpe is 5.6, against a measured 2.21 and a correlation ceiling near 3. Even granting the ceiling in full, the ratio would have to reach 5 — roughly double anything this study could produce, and the experiments that tried to push it further (pyramiding, trailing exits, continuous exposure) all cost more Sharpe than they bought in shape.

To reach the required Sharpe from ρ̄ ≈ 0.10 you would need per-signal Sharpe near 3.5 — nothing in 116 screened features came within a factor of three of that — or ρ̄ near 0.01, which means signals that are genuinely independent of one another, which on a single instrument they cannot be. The gap is filled either by a wide cross-section of instruments, which is outside a BTCUSDT-only brief, or by sub-second market making, where the edge is spread capture and the bet count runs to millions. Section 8 shows both halves of that second door measured and closed for anyone without co-location and a rebate tier.

  • Indicators for bar t use only bars ≤ t. A signal formed at the close of a 4h bar executes at the open of the next 15-minute bar, never earlier.
